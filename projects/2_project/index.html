Breathing Phantom Simulator

A Pneumatically Actuated Abdomen Phantom for Testing Robotic Ultrasound on a Moving Body

Organization: Healthcare Technology Innovation Center (HTIC), IIT Madras Research Park, Chennai
Role: Robotics Engineer · 2026

Real patients breathe. With every breath the chest and abdomen rise and fall, so the surface a robotic ultrasound system presses against is always moving. A static phantom cannot show whether the robot holds steady probe contact through that motion; testing on volunteers is not where an unproven controller should be tuned.

I built a breathing phantom simulator: a compact, pneumatically actuated test bed whose soft surface rises and falls like a breathing abdomen of a pregnant person. It gives the force-controlled robot a repeatable, moving target to test against before it ever touches a person.

System Overview

The phantom is built from three subsystems:

  1. Soft Breathing Surface: A flexible membrane stretched over a dome that inflates and deflates, giving a compliant, chest-like surface for the ultrasound probe to press on.
  2. Pneumatic Actuation: A motor-driven air pump pushes air through tubing into the enclosure under the membrane, so the surface moves smoothly without any rigid parts that a probe could hit.
  3. Microcontroller Control: A microcontroller drives the pump, setting the inflate–deflate cycle that defines the “breathing” pattern.

Design Evolution

First Prototype: Proving the Concept

First prototype: the DC air pump inflates and deflates the dome on a sealed enclosure to simulate breathing.

The first version answered one question: can a small pump and a sealed enclosure produce a convincing, repeatable rise-and-fall motion? It used a small domed surface on top of a box enclosure, a DC motor-driven air pump connected by tubing, and breadboard electronics. A fiducial marker is fixed to the top of the enclosure.

Final Version: Built for Robot Testing

The final version scales the idea up to something the robot can actually scan:

  • Larger, softer breathing surface: A wide membrane dome mounted in a rigid black housing gives the probe a big enough area to sweep across, closer to the size of a real abdomen.
  • Tested with the robot in the loop: The robot lowers the ultrasound probe onto the moving surface and has to keep contact while the surface breathes, a direct test of the admittance controller.

Why It Matters

A breathing surface turns force control from a static problem into a disturbance-rejection problem. As the surface rises, contact force spikes unless the robot yields; as it falls, the probe lifts off unless the robot follows. The phantom lets us see both failure modes safely and tune the controller until the probe rides the motion with steady contact.

Technical Stack

  • Mechanical: Domed membrane surface, rigid housing, soft materials
  • Actuation: DC motor-driven air pump, pneumatic tubing
  • Electronics: Microcontroller-based pump control, pressure sensors
  • Use: Validation test bed for the force-controlled tele-ultrasound robot
